How the AIs Work

This page explains how the artificial players ("AIs", or brains) on this site decide their moves. It is a companion to the User's Guide; you may want to read the description of the control column first, since several controls there (the depth menus, the brain editor, Evolve, Compete and Tournament) only make sense once you understand what a brain is.

A brain is nothing more than a list of numbers, called parameters or weights. Those numbers tell the AI how much it should care about each of a couple of dozen features of a position — the race, blots, primes, the golden points, and so on. Two things then turn those numbers into actual play: a static evaluation that scores any board position, and a lookahead search that uses that score to choose among the legal moves. We take them in that order, then list the parameters and give a table of the current values.

Lookahead: How Deep the AI Thinks

On its turn the AI looks at every legal way to play its dice and picks the one that leads to the best position. How far ahead it looks is set by the Depth menus in the control column (one per player, and one for batch runs), measured in plies. A ply is a single half-move — one player's turn. The depth is how many plies the AI reads before it falls back on the static evaluation to judge the resulting board.

  1. Depth 0 — no thinking at all: the AI simply plays the first legal complete turn it finds. This is a deliberately weak opponent, useful if you just want an easy game.
  2. Depth 1 (one ply) — the AI applies the static evaluation to every board it could reach with a legal play of the current dice, and chooses the move that yields the best-scoring board. This is very fast — instant for human play, and quick enough for whole tournaments and evolution runs — and it is the default.
  3. Depth 2 (two ply) — the AI looks one turn further. For each of its own candidate moves it considers all of the opponent's possible replies. Because the reply depends on dice that have not been rolled yet, it averages the value over all 21 distinct rolls, each weighted by how likely it is (a double is half as likely as a given non-double). The move with the best expected value is chosen. Two-ply play is a few percentage points stronger than one ply, but it takes a few seconds per move.
  4. Depth 3 and beyond — the same idea, one turn deeper each time. The cost grows very quickly: a single three-ply decision can take tens of seconds, so it is practical only for the occasional analysed position, not for a full game.

Because the dice are random, the correct way to search is not the familiar minimax of chess but expectimax: the tree alternates between decision nodes, where a player chooses the best move, and chance nodes, where we average over the dice. The whole tree is scored with the deciding brain's own static evaluation, from White's point of view: White tries to maximise the score and Red tries to minimise it. A win or loss (all fifteen checkers borne off) is treated as a value so large that it always outranks any ordinary position.

To keep the game responsive, searches of depth two or more are farmed out to several Web Workers (one per processor core), so the browser never freezes while the AI thinks. Depths 0 and 1 are instant and run on the main thread.

The Static Evaluation

The static evaluation is the heart of a brain. It takes a single board position and returns one number: how good that position is for White. Every feature is measured for both sides and entered as a difference, White minus Red, so the whole score is automatically anti-symmetric: swap the colours and the score simply changes sign. Writing $w_i$ for the parameters and $f_i$ for the features, the score is

$ \displaystyle S \;=\; \sum_i w_i\,\bigl(f_i^{\,\text{White}} - f_i^{\,\text{Red}}\bigr). $

A positive score is good for White, a negative one good for Red. Each brain is normalised so that its largest weight (in absolute value) is exactly 1000; this lets brains be compared on a common scale and keeps the doubling thresholds meaningful. The score is rounded to a whole number, rounding halves away from zero so that the anti-symmetry is exact.

Not every feature matters at every stage of the game. Once the two sides have passed each other and can no longer be hit — a pure race — blots cannot be hit, primes trap nobody, and made points and anchors block nobody. All of those terms therefore switch off automatically once contact is broken, and the AI's attention shifts entirely to the race: bearing checkers off and, when it is hopelessly behind, rushing its stragglers home to avoid the extra loss of a gammon or backgammon.

A couple of small adjustments are made only when the AI actually chooses its move, and are not part of a position's stored value: a disengagement nudge (DE) that encourages breaking contact and racing when ahead, and, once disengaged, a tiny tie-break that prefers advancing the rearmost checker when two moves would otherwise score identically.

The Parameters

The features below are the ingredients of the static evaluation. Most are self-explanatory once named; the sign of a weight says whether more of that feature is good (positive) or bad (negative) for the side that has it.

The Race

PCPip count. How far each side still has to travel. Carries a negative weight, so being ahead in the race is good.
BOBorne off. Checkers already taken off the board. Positive: bearing off is progress, and it distinguishes taking a checker off from merely stacking it deeper at home.

Mobility

EC1Encumbrance, one move. Of the 36 dice rolls, how many leave the side with only a single legal move. Negative: being cramped is bad.
EC0Encumbrance, no move. Rolls that leave the side with no legal move at all (a "dance"). Negative.

Containment and Freedom (the primes)

Each checker is graded by its escape count: how many of the six die-values would let it get past the front of an opposing wall that contains it. The six buckets run from completely trapped to nearly free:
F0Sealed — entombed behind a full prime, or closed out on the bar. The worst place to be.
F1–F5Increasingly free: one, two, ... five of the six dice let the checker escape. (A fully free checker, "F6", carries no weight.)
BEBar entombment. An extra penalty for a checker stuck on the bar, priced by how many turns it is expected to stay frozen there, because it paralyses the whole army until it enters.

Made Points

HBHome-board points. Points made in one's own home board, excluding the golden 4- and 5-points (which have their own weights).
G5The golden point — the 5-point, the single most valuable point to make.
G7The bar point — the 7-point.
G4The 4-point.
ANAnchors. Points held in the opponent's home board (excluding the golden anchor), counted only when behind in the race.
GAThe golden anchor — the opponent's 5-point, held from the back; also counted only when behind.

Blots (exposure and threats)

A blot is a lone checker that can be hit. The same event is an exposure for its owner and a threat for the other side, so four weights cover it: Direct versus Indirect shots, and blots in the Outer (far) boards versus one's own near (Point) boards.
DO / IODirect / indirect shots at a blot in the far part of the board.
DP / IPDirect / indirect shots at a blot in the near part of the board.

Move-selection heuristics

DEDisengagement. A bonus applied only when actually choosing a move: it rewards breaking all contact and racing when ahead (and discourages it when behind). It is not part of a stored position's value.

The Doubling Cube

Two further numbers govern the cube, in the same units as the score. Let own score be the static score seen from the side to move.
DTDouble threshold. The side offers a double (or redouble) when its own score rises above DT.
ATAccept threshold. The side accepts an offered double unless its own score has fallen below −AT.

A sensible take-window needs AT larger than DT; evolved brains vary, and a few even invert it — an aggressive doubler that is also a tight taker.

A glance down the table tells a story. Origin, the hand-tuned baseline, pours its weight into hitting (DO = 1000) and ignores containment entirely. The evolved brains do the opposite: they hold hitting to almost nothing and instead prize the race (PC), bearing off (BO), and the golden 5-point (G5) — which is why they beat the original so decisively.
